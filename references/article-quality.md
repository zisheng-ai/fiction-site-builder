# Article & Copy Quality

Apply this reference when writing or editing non-fiction prose for the fiction site:
- Traffic articles (`articles/` directory)
- Book taglines and synopses (in `books.ts`)
- Site copy (about, contact, homepage blurbs)
- Meta descriptions

For **fiction narrative prose** (novel chapters), use `story-deslop.md` instead — different domain, different rules.

Source: adapted from [stop-slop](https://github.com/hardikpandya/stop-slop) by Hardik Pandya (MIT).

---

## Core Rules

1. **Cut filler openers.** State the point first. Remove throat-clearing before the claim.
2. **Break binary contrast patterns.** State Y directly — drop the "not X, it's Y" setup.
3. **Active voice only.** Name the human actor. Passive voice hides who did what.
4. **Be specific.** No vague declaratives. Name the thing, not its importance.
5. **Put the reader in the room.** "You" beats "People." Specifics beat abstractions.
6. **Vary rhythm.** Mix sentence lengths. Two items beat three. No em dashes.
7. **Trust the reader.** Cut softening, justification, hand-holding.
8. **Cut quotables.** If it sounds like a pull-quote, rewrite it.

## Phrases to Remove

### Throat-Clearing Openers

Remove these; state the content directly.

- "Here's the thing:" / "Here's what [X]" / "Here's why [X]"
- "The uncomfortable truth is" / "The real [X] is"
- "It turns out" / "Let me be clear" / "The truth is,"
- "I'm going to be honest" / "Can we talk about"

Any "here's what/this/that" construction = throat-clearing before the point. Cut and lead with the point.

### Emphasis Crutches

Delete — add no meaning.

- "Full stop." / "Period." / "Let that sink in."
- "This matters because" / "Make no mistake" / "Here's why that matters"

### Adverbs

Kill all adverbs. No -ly words, no softeners, no intensifiers.

Specific offenders: really, just, literally, genuinely, honestly, simply, actually, deeply, truly, fundamentally, inherently, inevitably, interestingly, importantly, crucially.

Also cut: "At its core" / "In today's [X]" / "It's worth noting" / "At the end of the day" / "When it comes to" / "In a world where" / "The reality is"

### Business Jargon

| Avoid | Use instead |
|-------|-------------|
| Navigate (challenges) | Handle, address |
| Unpack (analysis) | Explain, examine |
| Lean into | Accept, embrace |
| Landscape (context) | Situation, field |
| Game-changer | Significant, important |
| Deep dive | Analysis, examination |
| Moving forward | Next, from now |
| Circle back | Return to, revisit |

### Meta-Commentary

Remove self-referential asides. Let the piece move — don't announce its own structure.

- "Let me walk you through..." / "In this section, we'll..." / "As we'll see..."
- "But that's another post" / "The rest of this essay explains..."

### Vague Declaratives

Sentences that announce importance without naming the specific thing. Replace with the specific thing.

- "The reasons are structural" / "The implications are significant"
- "This is the deepest problem" / "The stakes are high"

## Structures to Avoid

### Binary Contrasts

State Y directly. Drop the negation entirely.

| Pattern | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| "Not because X. Because Y." | "Because Y." |
| "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is." | "Y is the problem." |
| "The answer isn't X. It's Y." | "The answer is Y." |
| "not X, it's Y" / "isn't X, it's Y" | State Y. |
| "not just X but also Y" | "X and Y." or just "Y." |

### Negative Listing

Listing what something is not before revealing what it is. State Z; cut the runway.

- "Not a X... Not a Y... A Z." → "Z."
- "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z." → "Z."

### Dramatic Fragmentation

Sentence fragments for emphasis = manufactured profundity.

- "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]." → Complete sentence.
- "This unlocks something. [Word]." → State the thing.

Fix: Complete sentences. Trust content over presentation.

### False Agency

Inanimate things doing human verbs. Name the actual human actor.

| Avoid | Fix |
|-------|-----|
| "the decision emerges" | "Someone decided" |
| "the culture shifts" | "People changed behavior" |
| "the data tells us" | "Readers who finished chapter 1 are more likely to..." |
| "the market rewards" | "Buyers pay for..." |
| "the complaint becomes a fix" | "The team fixed it" |

### Narrator-from-a-Distance

Put the reader in the room, not above it.

- "Nobody designed this." → "You don't sit down one day and decide to..."
- "People tend to..." → "You tend to..."
- "This happens because..." → Name who did it and when.

### Passive Voice

Find the actor. Put them at the front.

- "X was created" → Name who created it.
- "It is believed that" → Name who believes it.
- "The decision was reached" → Name who decided.

### Sentence Starters to Avoid

| Avoid | Fix |
|-------|-----|
| Starting with What, When, Where, Which, Who, Why, How | Lead with subject or verb |
| Paragraphs starting with "So" | Start with content |
| Sentences starting with "Look," | Remove |

"What makes this hard is..." → "The constraint is [specific thing]."

## Rhythm Patterns

| Avoid | Fix |
|-------|-----|
| Three-item lists | Two items or one |
| Every paragraph ending punchily | Vary endings |
| Em dashes anywhere | Remove. Use commas or periods. |
| Stacked short punchy sentences | Mix lengths |
| "Not always. Not perfectly." | One direct statement |

---

## Quick Checks

Before delivering any article or copy block:

- Any adverbs? Kill them.
- Any passive voice? Find the actor, make them the subject.
- Inanimate thing doing a human verb? Name the person.
- Sentence starts with a Wh- word? Restructure it.
- Any "here's what/this/that" throat-clearing? Cut to the point.
- Any "not X, it's Y" contrasts? State Y directly.
- Three consecutive sentences match length? Break one.
- Paragraph ends with punchy one-liner? Vary it.
- Em dash anywhere? Remove it.
- Vague declarative? Name the specific thing.
- Meta-joiners ("The rest of this article...")? Delete.

## Scoring

Rate 1–10 on each:

| Dimension | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| Directness | Statements or announcements? |
| Rhythm | Varied or metronomic? |
| Trust | Respects reader intelligence? |
| Authenticity | Sounds human? |
| Density | Anything cuttable? |

Below 35/50: revise.

## Application in This Skill

### Traffic Articles (`articles/`)

Articles are the cold-traffic entry point. Sloppy prose = high bounce = wasted ad spend.

- Lead with the conflict or hook — no setup paragraphs explaining what the article covers.
- Every paragraph earns its place. If cutting it doesn't break the argument, cut it.
- Short paragraphs win on mobile. 1–3 sentences, then break.
- The reader came from a Facebook ad about a book — reward that expectation fast.

### Book Taglines

Taglines drive CTR and are the most compressed copy in the product. Apply the rules ruthlessly:

- No adverbs, no em dashes, no binary contrasts.
- 8–12 words max. Every word must do work.
- End on unresolved tension — never on a summary or conclusion.
- Bad: "He never expected to fall for her, but love has a way of finding us." → passive voice, vague declarative, cliché.
- Better: "He bought the company. She came with the deal." → specific, active, tension unresolved.

### Book Synopses (description field in `books.ts`)

- Lead with the conflict, not the setup.
- Introduce the protagonist and the obstacle in sentence 1–2.
- No throat-clearing ("In a world where..." / "Meet [Name], a woman who...").
- 3–5 sentences total. End on what's at stake, not a summary.

### Meta Descriptions

- ≤155 characters.
- State what the book / article is about in one active sentence.
- Include the genre signal or emotional hook.
- No "Click here to read" / "Find out what happens."
